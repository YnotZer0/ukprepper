# Urine: keep it separate (and stop the ammonia smell)

## Why separate urine from solids
- Smell. Most toilet smell comes from mixing the two. Bacteria in faeces carry an enzyme (urease) that quickly turns the urea in urine into ammonia, often within hours. Kept apart, the solids stay drier and smell much less, and fresh urine on its own smells far less until it starts to break down.
- Volume. Most of what a person passes each day by volume is urine, roughly 1-1.5 litres. Diverting it means the pits fill much more slowly and stay dry.
- Flies. Flies breed in wet waste. A dry, covered pile gives them far less to work with.
- Safety. Most of the germs that cause illness are in faeces, not urine. Separation keeps the risky material small, dry and contained.
- Easier work. There are no heavy, sloshing wet buckets, and the compost breaks down better without being waterlogged.
- A resource. Urine carries most of the nitrogen we excrete. Diluted, it feeds trees and shrubs.

## Set-up for low smell
1. Divert at the source: a urine-diverting seat insert, or a funnel at the front of the seat. Everyone sits, which cuts splashing a lot.
2. Use a short, smooth pipe (32-40 mm) with a steady fall all the way. Urine left sitting in a dip goes stale and smells.
3. Seal the tank. A 25 L jerrycan works well. Run the inlet pipe down to near the bottom, so the urine itself forms a seal that stops air coming back up the pipe.
4. Fit an overflow near the top of the tank, running on with a steady fall to the soakaway trench. Once the tank is full it empties itself - nothing to carry - while the full tank keeps acting as the smell trap.
5. Keep the tank cool and shaded, and its cap closed. Warmth speeds up ammonia release.
6. Stand the tank on a tray, and check the joints for drips.

## Daily and weekly habits that cut the ammonia smell
- After use, rinse the diverter with a small cup of water and a splash of white vinegar.
- Wipe the seat and diverter daily with a vinegar spray.
- Weekly: pour a kettle of warm water with a cup of vinegar down the diverter to flush the pipe.
- Monthly: descale. Urine leaves a hard mineral scale ("urine stone") in diverters and pipes, which holds smell and bacteria. Soak or flush with citric acid or strong vinegar, then scrub.
- Once a month, add a cupful of vinegar or a tablespoon of citric acid to the tank. Acidity slows the conversion of urea to ammonia and helps keep scale down. The amount is a starting point - adjust it to your results.
- Every few months, disconnect the tank, rinse it out into the trench with water and vinegar, and refit it.

! NEVER mix bleach with urine or use bleach in the diverter, pipe or tank. Bleach and ammonia release toxic chloramine gas.

## The soakaway trench
- Dig it on the raised area: about 30 cm deep, 30-40 cm wide and 2-3 m long. Keep it out of the nut-drop zone, for example along the foot of the wall.
- Lay a perforated pipe (or a pipe with holes drilled along it) along the middle, and fill around it with wood chip and chopped branches - the branches from the old hole are ideal.
- Top it up with fresh wood chip or straw every month. The carbon soaks up the nitrogen, and soil life uses it to break the wood down.
- The trench also takes washing-up and washing water, which keeps it working.
- If it ever ponds or smells, extend it or start a second length.

Using urine as fertiliser instead: dilute about 1 part urine to 10 parts water, and pour it onto the soil around ornamental trees and shrubs. Move to a different spot each time, and don't apply it in heavy rain or on frozen ground.

Rules:
- Keep it out of ditches, drains, ponds and wells.
- Keep it off food crops, and off the ground where you pick up nuts.
- Wash your hands after handling the tank or pipe.

## Smell checklist
If you can smell ammonia, check these in order:
1. Is the tank cap or vent loose?
2. Is there scale in the diverter or pipe?
3. Is urine sitting in a dip in the pipe?
4. Is the tank in the sun?
5. Is the overflow or trench blocked or ponding?
